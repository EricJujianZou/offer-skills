# Examples and recaps

The last section holds examples that are not from the book.

## The definition

The book defines an offer as three things: the goods and services the seller provides, how the seller takes payment, and the terms of the deal. It grades offers on a ladder from no offer, through bad, decent and good, up to a grand slam offer, with profit rising at each rung.

A grand slam offer combines an attractive promotion, a value proposition no competitor matches, a premium price, a strong guarantee, and payment terms that let the seller collect cash from new customers before spending much to serve them. Because no alternative is comparable, the seller can sell in a vacuum: the buyer weighs the offer against doing nothing, and the price follows the value the buyer perceives. The book traces the idea to the advice that an offer should be so good that buyers would feel stupid saying no.

## Commoditized against differentiated

- A commoditized offer is a product the buyer can get from many places. When the options look the same, the buyer treats the cheapest as the best choice, and the purchase is driven by price.
- A differentiated offer has no direct comparison, so the purchase is driven by value. The book calls this selling in a category of one.

The book sums up the weak agency offer as a deal where the client pays for work, the agency does the work, and results may or may not follow.

## Before and after: lead generation agency

This case comes from section I of the book, and this skill is its home across the offer skills. The book's example is a software company that serves advertising agencies, which in turn sell to brick and mortar businesses. The numbers are rounded.

- Before: the agency charged $1,000 up front and a $1,000 monthly retainer. Ads returned 0.5 to 1 times their cost, so the agency lost money on each new client and broke even only after the second month.
- After: the client paid once with no retainer and covered only ad spend. The agency generated the leads, followed up on them, charged only for people who showed up, and promised 20 shows in the first month or the next month free. Becoming a client also came with daily sales coaching for staff, tested scripts, tested prices and offers, recorded sales calls, and the full playbook for the client's industry.
- Result: for the same ad spend and the same views, the agency got 2.5 times the responses, 2.5 times the closes and an upfront price 4 times higher. The book reports this as 22.4 times more cash collected up front, $10,000 spent to collect $112,000. (2.5 times 2.5 times 4 is 25; quote the book's figures as the book's and do not recompute them in copy for the user.)

## Recap of the method

The book's closing lessons, keyed on the user's situation:

| The user's situation | Lesson | Sibling skill |
|---|---|---|
| Buyers compare the offer with cheaper look-alikes | Avoid being a commodity; pick a growing market and narrow to a niche | offer-market |
| The price was set by copying competitors | Charge a premium price | offer-market |
| Buyers do not see why the product is worth the price | Raise the four value drivers and build the core offer in five steps, keeping delivery profitable | offer-build |
| Buyers say "maybe later" | Use urgency to lower the point at which buyers act | offer-urgency |
| Buyers see unlimited supply | Use an honest limit to raise the price buyers accept | offer-scarcity |
| Buyers say it is too expensive | Add bonuses to raise demand without cutting the price | offer-bonuses |
| Buyers fear it will not work for them | Remove the risk with a guarantee, which also gets more buyers to use the product | offer-guarantees |
| The offer has a generic name or stopped drawing a response | Name it so it draws the right buyers and turns away the wrong ones | offer-naming |

## Examples for small software and service products

This example shows the Build mode handoff for a small paid Chrome extension.

- Product: a Chrome extension that drafts replies to customer support emails for small online stores.
- Before: "ReplyPro, $9 per month", with a feature list. A buyer compares it with free AI chat tools and sees no reason to pay.
- `MARKET`: niche is single-owner online stores answering 30 or more support emails a day. Pain passes (hours lost daily), purchasing power passes (the store already pays for other tools), easy to target passes (store owner forums and platform app stores), growing passes if marketplace listing counts for the store platform rose over three years. Price position: premium at $49 per month.
- `CORE OFFER`: dream outcome is "inbox cleared before lunch every day". The core offer stack adds a one-time setup call that loads the store's policies (high cost tier), a reply template library per question type (low), and a weekly report of repeated questions (low), each with a stated value.
- Enhancers: limited supply on setup calls per week, rolling cohorts for onboarding, bonuses chosen with offer-bonuses' wow test, a conditional guarantee tied to installing the extension and loading the policies, and a MAGIC name such as "Store Owner's Clear Inbox by Noon Kit".

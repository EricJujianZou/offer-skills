# Why the enhancers come after the core offer (chapter 11)

The book calls scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees and naming the enhancers: parts added around the core offer that raise demand without changing what is delivered. They run after offer-build because they raise demand for a core offer that already solves the buyer's problems; they cannot stand in for one.

The reasoning is supply and demand. More demand sells more units, and lower supply, real or perceived, sells each unit for more. Each enhancer moves one side:

| Enhancer | What it changes |
|---|---|
| Scarcity | Lowers perceived supply, which raises the price buyers accept and adds demand through exclusivity. |
| Urgency | Raises demand by lowering the point at which a prospect acts. |
| Bonuses | Raise demand and perceived exclusivity. |
| Guarantees | Raise demand by removing the buyer's risk. |
| Name | Restarts demand and widens awareness among the target buyers. |

## The user plans to serve every interested buyer in one round

Desire exists only while it is unmet. A seller who meets all demand at once sells fewer units at each later promotion, until nobody is left to buy. The book states the rule as: the longer the seller delays the ask, the bigger the ask can be. The agent keeps the user's supply below the demand the user can generate, and leaves some demand unmet for the next round.

Example: a seller promotes a two day workshop and sells 10 seats at $500 each, for $5,000. Repeating this sells fewer seats each round, because every interested buyer was already served.

## Some buyers would pay far more than the current price

Demand for a service is not spread evenly across buyers. About one fifth of prospects will pay five times the price or more. The agent offers a higher tier to that fifth before cutting price for the rest.

Example: the same workshop seller instead offers two one to one sessions at $5,000 each, for $10,000, and 8 interested prospects go unserved. The book prefers this: more revenue, lower delivery cost, and unmet demand that lets the next round open a few more spots at the same price. The 2 buyers out of 10 who pay ten times as much show the uneven demand.

## Demand is rising faster than the user can serve

The agent cuts or holds supply and raises the price instead of adding capacity at the old price.

Example: a charity was advised to cut supply when demand rose. It reduced the guest list to 100, raised the ticket price from $15,000 to $25,000, and raised close to $5,400,000 in one night.

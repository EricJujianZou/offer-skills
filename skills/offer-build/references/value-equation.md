# The value equation

This file holds the one full explanation of the value equation across the offer skills. Other skills state it in one sentence and point here.

## The formula

Value = (Dream outcome x Perceived likelihood of achievement) / (Perceived time delay x Perceived effort and sacrifice)

| Driver | Direction | Question the buyer is asking |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | Raise | What do I get at the end? |
| Perceived likelihood of achievement | Raise | How sure can I be that it will happen for me? |
| Perceived time delay | Lower | How long until I see the result? |
| Perceived effort and sacrifice | Lower | What do I have to give up or do along the way? |

Every driver is perceived. A change the buyer does not notice does not change value until the offer tells the buyer about it.

## Rules for each driver

**Dream outcome.** The dream outcome is the distance between where the buyer is now and the life the buyer imagines. The seller directs a desire that already exists and does not create one. The outcome that most raises the buyer's standing relative to other people is usually valued highest, so copy that describes how others will see the buyer's result tends to work. Two offers that promise the same outcome cancel out on this driver, and the other three drivers decide which is worth more.

**Perceived likelihood of achievement.** Buyers pay for certainty. Proof, track record, what is included or left out, and guarantees all raise it. The book's illustration: a surgeon's ten-thousandth patient gets the same operation as the first patient, and would still pay much more for it because of the track record.

**Perceived time delay.** It has two parts. The first is the long-term result the buyer is paying for. The second is the set of short-term wins that keep the buyer going long enough to reach it. Every offer should give the buyer a clear win as soon after purchase as possible. For example, a buyer who pays for a result worth $239,000 a year still needs a first $2,000 sale within seven days to stay committed.

**Perceived effort and sacrifice.** It covers every other cost the buyer pays along the way, tangible or not. Done-for-you service is priced above do-it-yourself because it removes effort and also raises the buyer's confidence in the result.

## The bottom half matters most

Beginners compete on the top half by making bigger claims, which any competitor can copy. Strong companies compete on the bottom half by cutting time and effort. As time delay and effort approach zero, value grows without limit, because the equation divides by them.

The book's speed comparisons: a private licensing office that renews a license for $50 with no queue, overnight courier against standard mail, a music app against slow free downloads, a ride app against walking. Its point is that speed is the one thing that beats a free alternative, so a product competing with free should get faster.

## Same dream outcome, different value

The book scores pairs of offers with the same promise on each driver:

- Fitness programs against liposuction. Buyers pay around $25,000 for liposuction with a tummy tuck and resist $100 per month for a bootcamp, because surgery takes an afternoon and the bootcamp takes 12 to 24 months of effort.
- Meditation against an anti-anxiety drug. Both promise calm. Scored 0 or 1 per driver, the drug wins on likelihood, time and effort, which the book gives as the reason it is a multi-billion dollar product.
- Supplements against gyms. Supplements sell about $123 billion a year and health clubs about $62 billion, for the same reason.

## Psychological fixes before logical ones

Logical fixes, such as making the product itself faster, are usually expensive and have often been tried. A change in how the wait or effort feels can be cheaper and raise value more. The book cites Rory Sutherland: a live map of when the next train will arrive raised London rider satisfaction more than faster trains would have, for millions instead of billions. Mirrors placed in elevators make the ride feel shorter.

## Working with what buyers value

Do not argue that buyers ought to value the slower or harder option. Build the offer around what they already value, which usually means less time and less effort.

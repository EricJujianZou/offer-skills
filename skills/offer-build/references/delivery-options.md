# Delivery options and trimming

## The six delivery variables

Vary each variable to generate delivery options for a solution in step 5. Pick the variables to vary first from the situation the user is in.

| Situation | Variable to vary | Options to list |
|---|---|---|
| The buyer doubts they will keep going | Buyer effort | Do it yourself (the buyer gets the material), done with you (the seller teaches and guides), done for you (the seller does the work) |
| One-to-one time is the main cost | Personal attention | One-to-one, small group, one-to-many |
| The buyer gets stuck between sessions | Response speed and hours | Support in business hours or around the clock; replies within 5 minutes, an hour or a day |
| The buyer needs live help but not in person | Live channel | In person, phone, email, text, video call, chat support |
| An explanation is repeated to every buyer | Recording format | Audio, video, written |
| The list looks thin, or the price is under pressure | The 10x and one-tenth test | Ask what the user would provide if the buyer paid ten times the price, or $100,000. Then ask how the product could become more valuable and still work at one-tenth of the price. |

The test for the full list is whether the buyer, reading it, would be surprised at how much is included and say yes.

## Cost tiers

Rate every option on one of three tiers. Do not ask the user for dollar figures.

| Tier | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| low | Automated, or a one-time asset that costs almost nothing per extra buyer | template, calculator, recorded walkthrough, software feature, prewritten checklist |
| medium | Group or batch effort shared across buyers | weekly group call, monthly batch review, cohort chat moderated once a day |
| high | One-to-one time or money spent per customer | private call, custom setup, personal review, buying something on the buyer's behalf |

## Trim rules

1. Rate each option's value to the buyer as high or low. An option is high value when the buyer would value it financially, would feel more certain of success, would spend much less effort, or would reach the result much sooner.
2. Cut high cost, low value options first.
3. Cut low cost, low value options next.
4. Keep low cost, high value options. These are the base of the stack.
5. Keep high cost, high value options, then step each one back a version at a time (one-to-one, then small group, then recorded) until its cost is acceptable. The alternative is to keep it and raise the price enough to pay for it.
6. Prefer one-to-many assets. They cost a lot once and almost nothing per extra buyer. Spend one-to-one time only on the items that add the most value.

The book illustrates rule 5 with an extreme case: moving in with a client to shop, cook and train with them would make the client certain of the result, but no realistic price would make it worth doing. Each step back from that version is cheaper, and the user stops at the first version with an acceptable cost.

## Order of operations: demand first, cutbacks later

The first version of an offer should over-deliver even when that is costly. Get buyers saying yes first, earn from that demand, and only afterwards cut back what is included or add friction. A done-for-you service can later be delivered as teaching instead, at lower revenue per client but to many more clients.

## Short book examples

- **Answer every objection.** A seller who insists that every buyer cook at home loses every buyer who eats out. Adding an eating-out guide turned that objection into a sale each time it came up.
- **Price far above cost.** A photography business raised its average sale from $300 to $1,500 over two years, which took weekly profit from $1,000 to $38,000.
- **One-to-many asset.** A meal planning spreadsheet that took about 100 hours to build then produced each personalised plan in about 15 minutes.

## Examples for small software and service products

**A Chrome extension that summarises long email threads.** Dream outcome: "Clear a 200-message inbox in 20 minutes." Problems by step: installing (worry about permissions, setup time), first use (does it work with this mail client), reading summaries (can the summary be trusted), acting on them (replying is still slow). Delivery options run from a recorded setup walkthrough and a link from each summary line to the source message (both low tier), through a weekly group question session (medium tier), to a private setup call per user (high tier). The trim keeps the automatic summaries, source links and walkthrough. It drops the private call, which is high cost and low value at a small monthly price, and keeps the group session as the stepped-back version.

**A freelance website service for local businesses.** Dream outcome: "A site that brings in booking requests within 30 days." Problems: writing the copy, gathering photos, connecting a booking tool, knowing whether the site works. Delivery options: a copy questionnaire (low), a batch photo day for several clients (medium), done-for-you booking setup (high), a monthly traffic report generated automatically (low). The trim keeps the done-for-you booking setup because it removes the largest effort, and raises the price to cover it.

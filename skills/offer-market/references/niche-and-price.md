# Niche pricing ladder and price cycle

## Niche pricing ladder

The book credits this ladder to Dan Kennedy. The core content stays nearly the same at every level. Only the title and the buyer change, and each narrower buyer can tie the product to a larger payoff per result, so each can justify a higher price.

| Product title | Buyer | Why this buyer pays more | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time management | Anyone | Generic, competes with many free options | $19 |
| Time management for salespeople | Salespeople | The course can be tied to closing one more deal | $99 |
| Time management for B2B outbound sales reps | Outbound reps selling to businesses | One extra sale earns the rep $500 or more | $499 |
| Time management for outbound reps selling power tools and garden equipment | Reps in one product category | The buyer sees it as built for their exact job | $1,000 to $2,000 |

A second pair uses the same logic: a general weight loss program at $19 and one built for nurses who work night shifts at $1,997, with the same diet and exercise core.

How to build a ladder for the user:

1. Start from the user's current broad title and price.
2. Add one qualifier per level (job, industry, situation, stage).
3. At each level, write what one more result is worth to that buyer in money.
4. Set the price as a fraction of that number. For a subscription, compare per year (12 times the monthly price) unless the user says otherwise.
5. Stop at the level where the user can still name three places to reach hundreds of these buyers. A narrower level that fails the easy to target test is too narrow.

## Price cycle

Use the row that matches what the user is considering. Each effect of a price cut reverses under a price rise.

| User is considering | Effect on buyers | Effect on the business | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cutting the price | Buyers commit less, value the product less, follow through less and get worse results. The cheapest buyers arrive and are the hardest to satisfy | Margin shrinks until there is no money to hire good people, improve the service or grow | Do not cut unless delivery costs about one tenth of competitors' cost |
| Raising the price | Buyers commit more, value the product more and get better results because they are invested. Better buyers arrive and cost less to serve | Margin grows and pays for systems, staff, a better customer experience and growth | Raise value first, then price, and check delivery evidence |
| Giving a discount | A discount teaches buyers that the list price is negotiable | Margin falls on every discounted sale | Offer a bonus instead, which keeps the price and adds value |

A higher price can also raise the value a buyer experiences with no change to the product, as in the wine test in references/book-examples.md.

## Examples for small software and service products

A Chrome extension that summarizes web pages sells to anyone who reads online at $3 per month. Dozens of similar tools cost $0 to $10, so it is commoditized.

| Title | Buyer | What one result is worth | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Page summarizer | Anyone | Little, many free options | $3/mo |
| Summarizer for recruiters | Technical recruiters | Faster screening of candidate pages | $15/mo |
| Candidate brief builder for agency tech recruiters | Agency recruiters paid per placement | One extra placement is worth thousands of dollars | $99/mo |

The four market tests for the last row:

- Pain: pass. Screening time limits how many candidates a recruiter can place.
- Purchasing power: pass. Agencies earn a fee per placement and already pay for sourcing tools.
- Easy to target: pass. Recruiter communities, LinkedIn groups and staffing association lists.
- Growing: check Google Trends for the category terms and the count of recruiting tools in the Chrome Web Store over the last three years. Rate it "unverified" until that check is done.
